Final project reflection:

Ultimately, over the course of this project, and semester at large, I can concisely say that I learnt a good amount. Of the things I did well, I do actually believe that there was a good amount - for the most part, I managed to get things finished and turned in by their due dates, and of the articles and so on I had to read, I tended to grasp the content rather quickly, for the most part. I also tended to put more effort into my work than may have been strictly necessary, at least in this course, as there were some minor questions I had that it was legitimately quicker to just produce multiple versions of an assignment and upload all of them, rather than send an email and wait for clarification. Sometimes my perfectionism also prompted me to keep adding to a project if I found it interesting, or had an idea that I thought was good enough to add - even if the project was technically already complete - although this wasn't a constant thing, as I needed to find the project either interesting, or the process of improving it interesting. And one final thing I think I did alright in this class, aside from attending most of the zoom lectures on time, or figuring out GitHub and using it semi-reliably, is that I ultimately managed to get into a habit of getting into the classwork, meaning that usually I would finish assignments given on the same day they were assigned, assuming it wasn't some massive project like the final, that is, meaning that when I did have to take more days on individual projects, I had more time I could spend on them without worrying about other assignments in this class.

As for the things I might to differently, If I could somehow retake this course? For starters, I would stop stressing as much about some assignments - most of the time, I would be worried about an assignment, and when I finally got around to doing it, it would usually be done rather quickly, at least in comparison to assignments from my other classes - and the same can be said for the reading assigned in this class. Aside from that, I would have probably tried to make my homepage look better if I had more time, as I will admit, I sort-of finalized it by halfway through the course, and just kept adding links to it afterwards - I will be the first to admit that it is a creation solely of function, and is not the most visually appealing. Admittedly, however, there isn't much I would change if I could somehow re-do this class, barring some minor changes, or getting my scheduling improved, or even just spending more time going over JavaScript to get a better grasp on it, as for the most part, this class went as well as I had hoped.

And as for the feedback on the project? I only got 8 suggestions, and since they were rather easy to implement, I did so to all of them. The improvements mainly consisted of cosmetic changes, such as making some headers clearly be distinct from links, make the text and photos bigger, make the links relevant for traveling the site bigger and more spread out, and rename the button to head to the bottom of the page - and that in truth, was all of them. Aside from those changes that I implemented more-or-less exactly as specified, there actually wasn't too much that I could think of implementing - Originally, I had planned to try and make the timeline or homepage contained within a slider, but something about the setup seemed to bug it, and admittedly, with only 2 days left at the time, I couldn't be bothered to spend the entire time trying to figure out what I had wrong in the settings, when I could instead be improving the project in other ways (I'm fairly confident however, that the issue was likely related to either the headers, or images being centered, or maybe even the border I used to separate the rovers). I spent most of the time making the site more visually appealing than the last draft was (Which was admittedly hideous), and in general trying to make it look more modern - and I (sort of) succeeded - instead of looking like it came from the 1980s, it now truly looks like a hobbyist website, which is certainly an improvement. Admittedly, I might have gotten a little to addicted to text shadows and hyperlinks - but in my defense, they both are rather versatile. But aside from that? While there are likely quite a few ways my project could be improved, for the first 'serious' webpage I have made, I would say I am actually rather proud - sure, it looks like a hobbyist's work, but considering I'm still rather new to web technology, I would take that as a compliment.

Thoughts on Progressive enhancements:

Based on what I read, and what I managed to gather from it, progressive enhancement seems to be a type of methodology for focusing on how to design a website - and whereas graceful degradation seems to focus solely on the newest browsers, progressive enhancement instead focuses on the content of web pages itself, rather than individual browser versions and devices.

Mainly, it just seems to focus getting the content right first, and then slowly switching out to the more complicated and taxing languages, first being CSS, and after that, JavaScript, rather than focusing on solely the newest browsers, and the features they can provide - a mindset that might make more use of modern technology, but also can severely handicap the experience on more limited, or even equally advanced but competing devices.

In terms for how this is relevant to modern web development, I would argue that it is because the lack of focus on the newest technology available and instead being on the content itself that makes it a great method. For starters, while not every browser can handle javascript, or in some cases even css, for the most part, html works on all devices. And if you start with making it look good at html, and then slowly start working to css, and then javascript, it will look as good as possible on a wide variety of devices. After all, if a website is built with the intention of looking good in javascript solely, then it likely will look terrible with just css, let alone html only, but if a website is built to look good in html, before moving onto looking good in css or javascript, then it will look pleasant (or at least tolerable), even on the devices unable to run javascript, or even css. After all, a link in html can be made to look beautiful in css or javascript, but a link in javascript or css only cannot be even accessed by someone who can only run html, let alone made to look nice.

The point I am getting at, is that progressive enhancement seems to focus on interoperability, before finally trying to make things more pleasant to look at. You start with a basic html website, and make sure it works in html, before you begin implementing css or javascript - and this works wonders for people on devices who cannot handle javascript or css, and lets them access it easily, even if it doesn't look as nice as it would with css or javascript. The same cannot be said for the graceful degredation method, because if you focus solely on the newest browsers, and latest technology, while you can make a website that looks pleasant, people with more restricted hardware will at best have issues interacting with the webpage, and at worst, have one or multiple key features of the website broken, as they rely on css or javascript to fix - and well, if your device can't handle either, then you are left with the broken page. And obviously, this requires the basic html skeleton of the website to be properly marked up in html, which in turn, will also make it more functional for both people who cannot use css or javascript, and for people who have disabilities, as html can allow for things such as screen readers to know alternative descriptions for images in the like, but if all the effort is put into javascript and css, that may not work properly, if at all.

Some additional reasons I believe that progressive enhancement is better than graceful degradation as well, is that the focus on content can also result in both parties benefitting - people with more limited devices and older technology can still enjoy the browser, while those with more modern browsers can still utilize implemented technology for improvements - and if the html is tagged especially well, that will also greatly benefit any css and javascript on the site, anyways. Or in other words, by focusing on making the content of the site, and its html work properly, and make it properly marked up, you not only can improve the ability for older and more restricted devices to access that content, but you also make it so that newer browsers with newer features have better marked up text to work up, and even potentially can end up aiding people with disabilities outright. After all, screen readers and plugins to prevent epilepsy are going to have a much better time working in html, especially if that html is formatted properly, rather than having to outright search all over the website for something, and even then, potentially getting it incorrect. Ultimately, properly formatted and marked-up html is a benefit for everyone, not just those on devices too weak or old to use the newest features.
